# Chapter Twenty-Five: What She's Carrying

"I'm pregnant."

She hadn't planned to say it like that - blunt, no lead-up, the words arriving out of her in the same desperate register as *don't leave* had a moment earlier. But once they were out there was no calling them back, and some part of her was almost relieved by that, relieved to have finally handed him the one fact she'd been carrying silently through everything else, the factory and the rope and the two days of dark she still hadn't fully surfaced from.

Satya went very still.

"You're sure."

"The doctor mentioned it while you were out of the room yesterday. Routine bloodwork." Her voice was steadier now than she'd expected it to be, though her hand hadn't loosened its grip on his. "I wasn't going to tell you like this. I wasn't going to tell you as a reason to stay. But you're sitting there deciding whether to leave, and I don't - I can't just let you decide that without knowing."

He didn't say anything for a long moment, his face unreadable in a way that frightened her more than any expression could have. When he finally spoke, it wasn't with the tenderness she'd braced herself, however foolishly, to hope for.

"You know what to do about that," he said, evenly, almost gently, which made it so much worse. "You've never liked anyone snatching your independence away from you. I'd have thought a baby would be the largest theft of it yet."

The words landed somewhere below her ribs, sharper than anything Aniket's hand had managed in that factory, because Aniket had never known her well enough to know exactly where to aim. Satya did. He was handing her back, deliberately, precisely, the same sentence she'd once used against her own father - *I only just learned how to be someone who makes her own decisions* - and using it now as a blade rather than the shield it had once been for her.

"Say that again," she said, very quietly.

"You heard me."

"You want me to end it. Is that what you're telling me."

"I'm telling you it's your choice. Entirely yours. Isn't that what you've always wanted - to decide these things for yourself, without anyone else's hand on the pen?" His voice stayed level throughout, no cruelty audible in the tone of it at all, which was exactly what made it cruel - a man weaponising her own convictions back at her with perfect, surgical calm.

She started crying then, properly, the sound of it raw and unpretty, everything the last two weeks had cost her finally finding an exit it could use. She didn't try to stop it. She didn't try to argue with him through it, either - some instinct told her that whatever this was, whatever test he was running on her, arguing would only prove whatever point he seemed determined to prove.

He let her cry. He didn't reach for her, didn't offer the easy comfort his hand had offered a minute earlier, just sat there and watched her come apart with an expression that gave away nothing about what it was costing him to keep watching rather than looking away.

What she didn't see, watching the floor rather than him, was his own hand closed white-knuckled around his knee, holding himself still the way he'd once held a needle steady in a corridor. Comfort now would have told her the cruelty wasn't real. He needed it to be real enough that whatever she said next, she said to a man who might actually leave.

It was several minutes before he spoke again, his voice changed entirely, all the flat cruelty gone out of it as suddenly as it had arrived.

"I'll marry you," he said. "I mean that properly this time - not the live-in compromise, not something I'm settling for because I don't want to lose the argument. Properly. On one condition."

She looked up at him through the mess of her own crying, not trusting herself to speak.

"You go to your parents. You ask their forgiveness - for the marriage you walked out of, for the better part of a year you've let them believe you hated them. I'm not asking you to become someone you're not. I'm asking you to close the door properly on the person you were running from, before you become someone's mother."

"You were testing me," she said, understanding arriving slowly, painfully. "Just now. The independence, the baby - you were testing whether I'd fight for this, or whether I'd let you push me away without a word."

He didn't deny it. "I needed to know you wanted this enough to survive me being cruel about it. I'm sorry for how I chose to find out."

She should have been angrier than she was. She found, instead, that she was simply exhausted past the point where anger had anywhere left to live in her - exhausted, and pregnant, and alive against odds she still hadn't fully processed, and looking at a man who had just proven, in the worst possible way, exactly how much he needed her to choose him back.

"I would do anything," she said, "for you to stay. Anything, Satya. I mean that."

He reached for her hand again, properly this time, and held it the way he had before any of this had started going wrong.
