# Chapter Four: A Look Across the Bar

By the time the first proper heat of summer settled over the city, Manaswini had stopped counting the weeks since she'd arrived and started, instead, counting the things that had become ordinary to her without her quite noticing when. The metro at rush hour. The particular sourness of Ipsita's kitchen mattress, long since traded for a paying-guest room of her own. And Friday nights at whichever Hauz Khas Village pub Zoya had decided on that week, usually on the strength of nothing more than a friend's friend saying the music was good.

Tonight it was good. Manaswini had on the short-pattern dress she'd bought with her first proper salary, still a little pleased with herself every time she wore it, and she was dancing the way she only ever danced with Zoya and two of the interns from the Bundelkhand desk - badly, happily, without the part of her mind that usually kept score of how she looked doing any of its usual work. Somewhere in the last six months, without a ceremony to mark it, she had become someone who could do this. She liked her a great deal, this version of herself.

She saw him between songs, in the gap where the DJ let one track bleed into the next. A man at a corner table, not dancing, not trying particularly hard to look like he wasn't watching the floor - watching her, specifically, with a steadiness that should have felt like staring and instead felt like something closer to being read. He didn't look away when she caught it. That, more than anything about his face, was what decided her.

"Who's the one who won't stop looking," she said to Zoya, not quietly enough.

"Which one?"

"Corner table. Green shirt."

Zoya followed her eyes, grinned, and said something about Manaswini finally showing interest in a man who wasn't a donor spreadsheet, which Manaswini ignored on principle and filed away as accurate.

She crossed the floor before she'd fully decided to. Up close he was younger than the table's stillness had suggested, dressed like every second software engineer in the city and somehow not blending into any of them, and when she said something forgettable about the music being better than the last three weeks' worth, he answered with an attention that made the forgettable thing feel, briefly, worth having said.

"Satya," he told her, when she asked.

"Manaswini."

"I know. Your friend said it twice, loudly, about four minutes ago."

She laughed before she could decide whether to be embarrassed, and something in his own face loosened slightly in response, the first real give she'd seen in it. She kept talking. She found, without particularly trying, that she wanted to keep talking - about the pub, about her job, about nothing that mattered, purely for the pleasure of watching him listen like it did.

There was a man across from him she hadn't properly looked at until he cleared his throat, amused, at the two of them. "I do exist," he said. "Vishesh. His friend, evidently now surplus to requirements."

"Sorry," Manaswini said, not sorry at all.

"Don't be. I've had worse company than being ignored for." He raised his glass an inch, an ordinary, forgettable gesture, and said something about how Satya never talked to anyone this long at these things, usually left him doing the socialising for both of them - said lightly, the way you'd say it about any friend's habits, and then the conversation moved past him entirely and didn't come back.

It was only afterward, waiting for their table's second round, that anything close to a moment happened. A man two tables over, well past his limit, lurched into a waiter's tray and sent it crashing, glass and ice skidding across the floor in a wide bright arc, and the whole corner of the pub flinched and turned at once - Zoya included, halfway out of her seat with a hand pressed to her chest. Satya didn't. He glanced over once, unhurried, registered it as nothing more than a minor mess someone else would clean up, and turned back to Manaswini mid-sentence as though the crash had happened in another room entirely.

"You didn't even blink," she said, half teasing.

"Should I have?"

"Most people would."

He shrugged, the gesture doing more work than the words that followed it. "I don't startle easy."

She thought, for exactly as long as it took the waiter to sweep up the glass, that there was something almost restful about a man who didn't startle - and then the music changed, and Zoya was pulling her back toward the floor by the wrist, laughing, and she let the thought go the way she let most thoughts go that summer, without holding onto it long enough to ask why it had occurred to her at all.
