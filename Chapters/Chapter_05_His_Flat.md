# Chapter Five: His Flat

They left before Zoya noticed, or perhaps exactly when Zoya noticed and chose, with a wink Manaswini caught over Satya's shoulder, not to make anything of it. The auto ride to his flat took eleven minutes she remembered nothing of afterward except the wind and the particular quiet between them, neither of them filling it with the kind of talk that usually rushed in to cover an evening tipping into something else.

His flat was on the third floor of a block in Malviya Nagar, ordinary from the stairwell - someone's forgotten shoes outside 3B, a cycle chained to the railing - and unremarkable in a way she found she liked, the way you like a room that isn't performing anything for you. He didn't apologise for the mess there wasn't much of, didn't do the small nervous tidying men usually did in the two minutes before a door closed behind a woman for the first time. He simply let her in, put the kettle on out of some instinct that had nothing to do with either of them actually wanting tea, and then didn't reach for it again once she'd crossed the kitchen and put a hand flat against his chest instead.

"You're sure," he said. Not a question exactly. Something closer to giving her one last, deliberate chance to make it one.

"I've been sure since the pub," she said, and that was the last full sentence either of them managed for a while.

What passed between them afterward belonged to the two of them and stayed there - not because there was anything to hide, but because some things lose something essential the moment they're narrated, the way a held breath loses its meaning the instant you describe the holding of it instead of simply holding it. What mattered, in the version of that night either of them would have been able to put into words later, wasn't the mechanics of it. It was that somewhere in the space of an hour she stopped thinking, even once, about the marriage she'd fled or the mountain she'd walked down in the dark to get here - stopped, for the first time in longer than she'd admitted to herself, being someone running from something, and became instead, briefly and completely, someone simply present in a room with another person who wanted her there.

She woke before dawn to find him already awake beside her, watching the ceiling with the unhurried stillness of a man who did most of his real thinking in the dark, and she lay a while without speaking, listening to the flat's small night sounds settle around them - a tap somewhere dripping, a neighbour's television murmuring faintly through the wall, the city outside still not quite awake either.

"What are you thinking about," she asked eventually.

"You," he said, which surprised her less than the plainness with which he said it - no performance in it, no obvious reach for effect, just a fact he was reporting the way he might report the time.

"Just me?"

"Mostly you." A pause, the kind that in another man might have meant he was composing something clever, and in him seemed to mean only that he was choosing to say the true thing rather than the easy one. "I don't usually let anyone this far in this fast. I noticed that about myself somewhere around the second drink, and decided not to fight it."

She turned onto her side to look at him properly, the grey pre-dawn light doing him more favours than fairness required, and thought that she had never, in the six months since Delhi first opened its doors to her, felt quite this unguarded with anyone - not her hostel friends, not Zoya, not even the parents she'd left behind a hillside road in the dark to get away from.

"Good," she said. "Don't fight it."

He reached for her hand under the sheet and held it, and for a while neither of them said anything at all, content to let the silence do what talk usually didn't.

"Tell me something true," she said eventually. "Not the interview version. Something you wouldn't lead with."

He considered it longer than the question seemed to deserve. "I grew up an only child in a flat smaller than this one, in Ghaziabad. My mother wanted a doctor. I gave her an engineer instead, and she's still deciding whether to forgive me for it." A small, dry pull at the corner of his mouth. "Your turn. Something you wouldn't lead with."

She thought of the mountain road, the third step she'd learned to skip, her father's strange unfinished sentence about debts older than her that she still hadn't found a shape to fit. She wasn't ready to hand him all of it, not this soon, not lying in a bed she'd known for less than a night. "I ran away from home to get here," she said instead, choosing the truth that cost her the least. "Properly ran. In the middle of the night, down a mountain, so my parents wouldn't wake up and stop me."

He didn't look shocked, or pitying, or any of the things she'd half-braced for. He only turned his head on the pillow to look at her properly, and said, "Then I suppose we're both people who left something behind to end up in this flat," and let that sit between them without pushing for more than she'd offered.

Outside the window the first grey edge of morning began, slowly, to find its way into the room.
