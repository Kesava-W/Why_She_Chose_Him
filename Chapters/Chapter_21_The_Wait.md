# Chapter Twenty-One: The Wait

Satya sat where he'd been standing an hour ago, though he couldn't have said when the sitting had happened. The flat held its ordinary shapes around him - her mug still on the drying rack, the tea he'd made and not touched gone cold on the counter - and none of them meant anything any more except as evidence of a morning that had already ended badly before it got worse.

He'd called Vishesh four times. Three times Vishesh hadn't answered. The fourth time he had, for eleven seconds, long enough to say *working on it, stay where you are* before the line went dead on his end, not Satya's - a decision, not an accident.

Stay where you are. As if there were anywhere else the last hour had left him fit to go.

He made himself think about the door instead of about her, because thinking about her produced nothing he could use. The door had been his decision, made alone, at six that morning, while she slept through it. An hour to reach Vishesh. An hour to make Aanchal stand down before she put herself in front of them. He'd told himself that was protection. Sitting here now, protection looked exactly like what it had always secretly been - a man deciding, again, that his judgment outweighed hers, and getting it wrong in a new and worse way each time he tried it.

He'd been wrong the same way once before, in a corridor, with a needle he'd told himself was the only door still open to him. He was starting to suspect that was simply the shape his love kept taking where she was concerned - a decision made instead of a question asked, every time it mattered most - and that he wouldn't get to find out, sitting in this flat doing nothing useful at all, whether today's version of that same old mistake had cost him the only thing he'd ever actually meant to protect.

The phone rang at eleven minutes past ten.

"Talk to me," Satya said, already on his feet.

"Not yet." Vishesh's voice had the flat, procedural calm Satya had learned, across the last year, to read as the sound of a man doing three things at once and giving away none of them. "I know roughly where. I don't have it exact."

"Roughly isn't-"

"Roughly is what I have. Get in your car. I'll call the second it's exact, and you will not beat me there by arguing about the word roughly."

Satya was already reaching for his keys.

"One more thing." A pause, brief enough that Satya almost missed the strain underneath the procedure. "Whatever happens in the next hour, don't ask me how I'm handling the other half of this. Not today. I need you focused on getting to her, not auditing my methods."

"Vishesh-"

"I mean it. Let me carry this one alone. You've carried enough."

The line went dead before Satya could push further, and some part of him - the part that had spent the last year deciding exactly what Manaswini was strong enough to be told - recognised, with an unpleasant clarity, precisely what this felt like from the other side of that particular door.

He drove without letting himself think too hard about what "roughly" could still cost.

The city didn't care. Traffic moved at its ordinary Tuesday pace, indifferent to the particular hour eating itself alive inside his car - a scooter cutting too close at a signal, a vendor calling out to no one in particular, the whole enormous machinery of Delhi going on exactly as it always did, unbothered by the fact that somewhere inside it, a woman he loved was being held by people who had already decided what her life was worth to them.

His phone buzzed once. A location pin, no message attached. He didn't wait to ask what it meant. He turned the car toward the city's outer edge, past the point where the roads stopped bothering to name themselves, and didn't call Vishesh back, because there was nothing left to ask that wouldn't cost time he didn't have to spend.

He didn't know, driving, whether he'd be in time. He didn't know what "roughly" had actually bought her, or what it had cost anyone else to buy it. He knew only the one fact he'd built the last hour around, and held it the way a man holds a rope he can't afford to look down past - that somewhere ahead of him, past roads with no names, she still wasn't safe.

He kept driving.
