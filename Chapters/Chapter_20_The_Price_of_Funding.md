# Chapter Twenty: The Price of Funding

Revanth crossed the last stretch of factory floor unhurried, hands in his pockets, the same easy warmth in his walk that had once made him seem, to Manaswini, like exactly the kind of man who ran a charity out of genuine conviction. He stopped a few feet from the chair and looked at her a long moment - at the split lip, the reddening along her cheek where Aniket's hand had landed four times over - with an expression that had nothing of concern in it at all.

"That's enough," he said to Aniket, mild, almost bored, the way you'd correct a junior colleague for overrunning a meeting. "She's more useful to us conscious and coherent."

"She's given me nothing."

"She may have nothing to give." Revanth crouched, closing the distance Aniket had kept, and studied her face with an interest that made her skin crawl worse than the slaps had. "I believe you, actually. I don't think you knew what your boyfriend was doing when he did it. I think you're exactly as ordinary and unlucky as you look."

"Then let me go." Her voice came out cracked, barely her own. "Please, Revanth. Whatever this is, I'm not part of it."

"You were never meant to be part of it. That's rather the trouble." He smiled, and it was, exactly as she would remember it afterward, an evil thing to see on a face she'd once trusted completely - not theatrical, not cruel for its own sake, just entirely without warmth, a man calculating rather than feeling. "Aanchal doesn't run on donations alone, Manaswini. Donations pay the electricity and the donor lunches. The real money, the money that matters, comes from elsewhere. A private backer. Very wealthy. Very particular about how his investment gets protected."

"What does that have to do with me?"

"He's been watching this situation unravel for weeks now. Two of our people dead, no clean explanation for either, and an entire funding chain suddenly one clumsy mistake away from unravelling all the way back to him." Revanth's voice stayed even, conversational, the horror of it entirely in the content and none of it in the delivery. "He's decided the chain needs cauterising. And he's decided the cleanest way to do that is to make an example that reaches all the way back to where this started - your family."

The room seemed to tilt around her, worse than the sedative had. "My parents."

"He wants them dead, Manaswini. As proof the leak is being sealed at its root, and as a message to anyone else in his network who might get careless about what their people's families know. I argued against it, for what little that's worth to you now - it's untidy, it invites exactly the kind of attention we don't need. He wasn't interested in my objections."

"You can't. They have nothing to do with any of this. They're farmers, they live in a village you've never heard of, they don't even know what Aanchal is-" Her voice broke properly now, the fluency that had once earned her this very job abandoning her entirely, leaving only the plain, unpolished shape of a daughter begging. "My mother has never left Pithoragarh district in her life. My father thinks Delhi is a place people go and don't come back changed for the better. They wouldn't even understand what was happening to them."

"I know," Revanth said, and something in his voice, for just a moment, sounded almost like it agreed with her that this was monstrous. It didn't stop him saying the rest of it.

"I know that. He knows that too. It doesn't change what he's decided." Revanth stood, brushing invisible dust from his knees with a fastidiousness that turned her stomach. "He'll be here himself, later tonight, to see it done. He likes to watch the things he pays for actually happen. Some men are made that way."

She was crying now, openly, the fear for Pushpak and Sarita eclipsing every fear she'd had for herself since waking in this room - her mother's hands stilling on the tawa, her father's strange unfinished sentence about debts older than her, the two of them asleep in a house she'd walked away from in the dark without once imagining she was walking them toward something like this. "Please. I'll do anything. Tell me what you want from me and I'll do it, just don't let him touch them."

"I'm afraid that decision isn't mine to unmake," Revanth said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse than cruelty would have been. "It was never yours to bargain for either. I'm sorry, in whatever way that word still means anything from me to you. Truly."

He turned and walked back the way he'd come, unhurried, leaving her alone with Aniket and the sound of her own breathing and a fear so total it had, for the moment, burned every other feeling in her clean away.
