# Chapter Thirteen: The Lift in Mumbai

A week later, in Mumbai, Zoya let herself be fussed over by her mother for two straight hours and didn't once complain about it.

She'd taken the Friday off specifically for this - her parents' small flat in Chembur, the smell of her mother's fish curry filling the whole stairwell before she'd even reached the door, her father pretending not to be delighted to see her and failing at it within the first five minutes. She hadn't been back in three months, work at Aanchal eating into every weekend she'd meant to use for exactly this, and something in her had loosened the moment she stepped off the train, the particular relief of being, for a handful of hours, someone's daughter and nothing more complicated than that.

They talked about nothing that mattered. Her mother's knee, which was better. A cousin's wedding date, still not fixed. Whether Zoya was eating properly in Delhi, a question her mother asked at every visit and never quite believed the answer to. Zoya let herself be fed a second helping she hadn't asked for, laughed at her father's terrible joke about the neighbour's dog, and half-listened to her mother's usual complaint about the building's lift - too slow, too old, the society still arguing over whose turn it was to pay for the repair - without any particular reason to think about it twice. She left just after eight with a container of curry pressed into her hands that she knew, riding down in the lift, she wouldn't manage to finish before it went off.

The lift doors opened on the ground floor to a woman already standing inside it, waiting, unremarkable in every way that mattered - mid-thirties, plainly dressed, the kind of face you'd pass a hundred times in a Mumbai building and never once think to hold onto. Zoya stepped in without a second thought, the curry container warm against her palms, already thinking about the auto ride back to the station.

The doors began to close.

The woman moved before they'd finished. One hand caught Zoya's wrist and pulled, hard and sure, no wasted motion in it at all - not the clumsy grab of someone acting on impulse, but something closer to a single practised gesture, executed the way you'd execute a task you'd rehearsed until your body no longer needed your mind's permission to run it. Zoya's back met the mirrored wall of the lift before she'd made a sound worth the name.

What happened after that happened quickly, and quietly, the lift's slow crawl between floors giving it exactly as long as it needed and not a second more. Zoya fought - some part of her did, hands scrabbling at an arm gone iron-still across her throat, heels finding no purchase against a floor gone suddenly unfamiliar beneath her - but whatever the woman had planned for this had already accounted for that, had already decided how much give the fight would be allowed and given it none. The curry container hit the floor somewhere in the middle of it, its lid coming loose, the smell of it strange and out of place against everything else happening in that small mirrored box.

By the time the lift reached the ground floor again, it was over.

The woman stepped out first, unhurried, straightening her dupatta with the same economy of motion she'd used for everything else, and walked out through the building's front gate without breaking stride, without a backward glance, without anything in her bearing that a passer-by would have found worth a second look. She didn't run. She had, evidently, no need to. Somewhere behind her, a lift stood open on the ground floor with its light flickering faintly, spilled curry cooling slowly against tile that would need, in an hour or two, considerably more than a mop.

She was three streets away, in an auto she'd hailed with a perfectly steady hand, before she allowed herself to think, for the first time since stepping into that lift, about her own mother - gone barely three weeks now, one name among fifteen on a list the newspapers had already stopped printing - and about the particular, uncomplicated stillness that had settled over her the moment the job, whatever it had truly been for her, was finished.

She didn't cry. She hadn't expected to. She only sat very straight in the back of the auto, hands folded in her lap, and watched Mumbai go by the way you watch a city you've decided, for reasons entirely your own, to keep still believing in.
