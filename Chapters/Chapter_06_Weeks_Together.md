# Chapter Six: Weeks Together

The weeks folded into each other the way weeks do when no one is counting them, and Manaswini noticed the shift only in hindsight - a toothbrush that had migrated permanently to his bathroom shelf, a second key that had stopped feeling like a formality and started feeling like a fact, three nights out of every seven spent at the paying-guest room only because appearances still mattered to her landlady, not because either of them wanted the distance.

Sunday mornings had become theirs in a way neither of them had planned. He was particular about how the eggs were done - not fussy exactly, but exact, the way he seemed to be exact about most small physical things - and the first time she'd scrambled them her own way, faster and looser than he liked, he'd eaten the whole plate without a word of complaint and then, the following Sunday, quietly taken over the pan himself. She'd called him out on it, laughing, accused him of being the kind of man who'd rather do a thing twice than say a sentence about it once, and he'd conceded the point so easily, so without defensiveness, that the small argument dissolved into something warmer before it had properly become an argument at all.

"I'll let you ruin the eggs," he told her, sliding the pan across, "if you promise to complain about my driving instead. Fair trade."

"Your driving is fine."

"It isn't. You're just too polite to say so."

She wasn't, as it turned out, and told him exactly what she thought of his lane discipline the very next time he drove her to work, and he took it the way he took most things she gave him honestly - without flinching, filing it away rather than arguing it down.

The television stayed on most evenings now, more out of habit than interest, a low murmur behind whatever either of them was actually doing. It was on, unremarked, the night the anchor's voice sharpened into the particular register newsreaders kept for things that mattered more than the segment before it - intelligence inputs pointing to a heightened threat of explosive attacks in Mumbai and Hyderabad, security forces conducting citywide preventive sweeps, visuals of men in uniform moving through markets with dogs on short leads, checking bags nobody had thought twice about carrying an hour earlier.

Satya glanced up from his laptop, watched perhaps four seconds of it, and said, "Good, they should," in the tone of a man agreeing with a policy he had no particular stake in, before returning to whatever spreadsheet had his attention.

"You're not worried?" Manaswini asked, mostly making conversation, her feet in his lap while she scrolled through something on her own phone.

"Delhi's not on the list."

"This time."

"This time," he agreed, and that was the whole of it - a channel that would, on any other evening, have been changed within the segment for something less grim, left running a beat longer than usual purely because neither of them had reached for the remote, and then changed anyway, without ceremony, the moment a film came on that Zoya had been telling her to watch.

She thought nothing of it. There was nothing, that evening, to think. The threat named on the screen belonged to cities she wasn't in, aimed at people whose faces she'd never see, and she let it pass through the room the way she let most of the day's noise pass through it - present for exactly as long as it took to say something mild about it, then gone.

What stayed, afterward, wasn't the news at all. It was the particular quality of the rest of that evening - him rubbing absent circles into the arch of her foot without seeming to notice he was doing it, the film Zoya had recommended turning out to be genuinely funny, both of them laughing at the same unremarkable joke at the same moment and catching each other's eye over it, delighted for no reason either of them could have explained to a stranger. She thought, somewhere around midnight, drowsy against his shoulder with the credits rolling unwatched, that she had never in her life felt this simply, unremarkably happy - not dramatically, not the way songs described it, just steadily, the way a room stays warm once the heater's been on a while.

She fell asleep on the sofa before the credits finished. He didn't wake her. He turned the television off himself, some time later, and carried her to bed the way you'd carry something you'd already, without saying so to anyone, decided you meant to keep.
