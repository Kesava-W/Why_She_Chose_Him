# Chapter Twenty-Seven: The Officer Who Understood

The woman who came to take her statement introduced herself as Sub-Inspector Ritika Verma, and Manaswini, still three days from discharge, one arm still tender where the drip had sat longest, found nothing in the introduction to make her look twice.

"Routine," Ritika said, settling into the chair beside the bed with a notepad she barely glanced at. "For the file. What you remember from the factory, in your own time. There's no rush."

She was plainly dressed, unremarkable in the particular way some people managed to be unremarkable on purpose rather than by accident, and she listened to Manaswini's halting account of the abduction, the interrogation, the shooting, with an attention that felt, oddly, less like an officer building a case and more like someone who already knew most of the shape of the story and was only confirming the parts that mattered to her personally. She asked few questions. She wrote almost nothing down.

"You said a man arrived in a second car," Ritika said, when Manaswini reached that part. "After the first car was fired on. Do you know who he was?"

"No. I never learned his name. He kept shouting for his father." Manaswini studied her a moment, something in the stillness of the question catching her attention in a way the routine ones hadn't. "Why? Does it matter?"

"It might." Ritika's pen didn't move. "There was another death connected to this case. Around the same time as the blast. A woman named Zoya - I understand you knew her."

The name landed hard, even weeks distant from the grief itself. "She was my friend. She was murdered in Mumbai, in a lift, and nobody ever told me why, not really. Satya said Aanchal was involved somehow, funding people who shouldn't have been funded, but he's never told me the whole of it."

"No," Ritika said, quietly. "I don't imagine he has."

Something in the way she said it made Manaswini look at her properly for the first time since she'd sat down - not as a uniform taking a statement, but as a person, tired in a specific way that had nothing to do with a long shift. "You know something about it."

Ritika was quiet a moment, her pen finally still against the notepad. "My mother was in the car park the day of the blast," she said. "Buying vegetables. She wasn't political, wasn't connected to anything - she was just there, at the wrong hour, the way most of the fifteen were." She said it plainly, without asking for sympathy, the way you'd report a fact you'd long since stopped expecting anyone to properly absorb the weight of. "I think about her every day. Some days it's the only thing I think about."

"I'm sorry," Manaswini said, and meant it entirely.

"So am I. About Zoya, I mean." Ritika looked at her then, something careful and deliberate in the look, offering exactly as much as she'd decided to offer and not a word further. "Grief does strange things to people, Manaswini. It makes some of us capable of things we'd never have believed of ourselves, before. I don't think that's always wrong. I'm not sure it's ever entirely right, either."

Manaswini sat with that a long moment, something cold moving through her that she couldn't yet name properly - not accusation, not yet, only the faint outline of a shape she wasn't sure she wanted to finish seeing. "Did you know her? Zoya?"

"No," Ritika said. "I never met her."

She said it steadily, without hesitation, the practised ease of someone who had long since decided exactly how much of any given sentence the truth was allowed to occupy. Manaswini let it stand without pushing further, some instinct telling her that whatever sat underneath this conversation was not hers to demand the rest of, not today, not from a woman who had just told her, in the only language she seemed willing to use, that she understood exactly what it cost to lose someone to violence that made no sense.

"Thank you," Ritika said, closing the notepad without having written more than a line in it, "for your time. I don't think we'll need to trouble you again."

She stood, and at the door she paused, looking back at Manaswini with an expression that held, for just a moment, something that might have been recognition passing between two people who'd each been changed, irreversibly, by the same wide net of violence, neither one entirely a victim of it and neither one entirely innocent of what had been done in their name.

"Take care of yourself," Ritika said, and left before Manaswini found anything to say back to that at all.
