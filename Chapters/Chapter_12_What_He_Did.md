# Chapter Twelve: What He Did

The banging started less than a minute after the deadbolt went home - three separate fists, she thought, though she couldn't have said for certain, the door shuddering in its frame with each strike, a voice on the other side demanding, in a language too flat and businesslike for the anger it should have carried, that they open up.

Satya didn't open it. He stood with his back against the wall beside the frame, one arm out, holding her behind him without seeming to think about the gesture at all, his face turned toward the door with an attention that had nothing frightened in it. She would think, later, that this was the moment she should have understood something was wrong with the shape of his calm. She didn't think it then. Then, she was only afraid.

The banging stopped.

It didn't taper off, didn't give way to voices arguing or retreating footsteps. It simply stopped, all at once, followed by three flat sounds in quick succession - not loud, nothing like a gunshot, closer to the sound of something heavy and unresisting meeting a floor - and then nothing at all. No footsteps. No voices. The kind of silence that felt, somehow, more finished than the noise before it had been.

Satya waited a long moment before he moved. When he finally opened the door, just a crack, just enough to look, he closed it again almost immediately, and turned back to her with an expression she didn't yet have the vocabulary to read.

"They're not going to bother us again," he said.

"What does that mean? Satya, what's out there?"

He didn't answer straight away. He crossed to the sofa and sat down heavily, the first genuinely unsteady thing she'd seen him do since she'd known him, and when he finally looked up at her it wasn't with fear. It was with the particular exhaustion of a man setting down something he'd been carrying alone for longer than she'd known.

"You remember the corridor," he said. "Three nights ago. Akhilesh, walking you up."

"What does Akhilesh have to do with-"

"You saw it happen. You just didn't know what you were looking at." His voice stayed level, almost gentle, which frightened her more than shouting would have. "There's a needle small enough that a man barely feels it pass him. A dose small enough to take an hour, sometimes two, before anything shows. He'd already have been three streets from here by the time it started, and no doctor in Delhi would have looked twice at what killed him."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere in the flat a tap was dripping, absurdly ordinary, and she found herself listening to it rather than to him for as long as she could manage, as though the sound could hold the rest of the sentence off a little longer.

"You killed him," she said finally. "You killed Akhilesh."

"Yes."

No qualification. No plea for understanding. Just the single word, offered as plainly as he'd have confirmed a dinner reservation, and it was the plainness of it, more than the fact itself, that made her back up a step from him without deciding to.

"And the men outside-"

"The same. Aanchal sent them for you - I don't yet know exactly why, only that they think you know something you shouldn't. I couldn't let them take you." He said it the way he might report a decision made under time pressure, no room in it for doubt about whether it had been the right one. "The syringes are still down there. Three of them. And your fingerprints are on the box they came from - you picked it up for me at the pharmacy on Aurangzeb Road, three weeks ago. I told you they were for my knee."

"You used me."

"I kept you alive." For the first time something in his voice sharpened, not anger exactly, something closer to a man refusing to apologise for the only choice he could see. "If the police open that door and find three bodies and a box with your prints on it, there is no version of tonight where you walk away from this clean. Mine or not."

She sat down, because her legs had stopped being reliable, and put her face in her hands, and for a while neither of them said anything at all. When she finally spoke it came out smaller than she wanted it to. "I have to go to the police. I have to tell someone."

"Tell them what? That your boyfriend killed a man you can't prove existed with a weapon that's already gone, using evidence that points to you as easily as to me?" He said it gently, which was somehow worse than if he'd shouted it. "Go to Aanchal instead, if you want. Walk in tomorrow and tell Revanth exactly what you know. See how long you last there once he understands you're a liability instead of an asset."

She didn't answer. She sat with her face in her hands and let the tap keep dripping, and somewhere underneath the fear, quiet and unwelcome, she understood that she had no move left that didn't end with something she loved destroyed.

She didn't go to Aanchal the next day. She told them she was unwell. She told them the same thing the day after that, and the day after that, until eventually she stopped needing to tell them anything at all.
