# Chapter Eleven: Three Men on the Road

Two days had passed since Akhilesh's desk had stopped being empty in a way anyone at Aanchal wanted to talk about, and Manaswini had, mostly, let the office's strange quiet settle into the background of her days the way most unexplained things eventually did. Work continued. Donor calls continued. She walked the same stretch home from the auto stand she always walked, past the same tea stall, the same stray dog that had adopted the lane outside her building without asking anyone's permission.

It was an ordinary evening, right up until it wasn't.

The car was already crawling the kerb before she registered it as anything other than traffic. A white sedan, windows part-down, pacing her at walking speed for three, four seconds longer than any car had reason to. She'd half-noticed and half-dismissed it - Delhi traffic did strange things at this hour, cars slowing for a hundred reasons that had nothing to do with her - when the door nearest her swung open and a man was already out of it, already reaching.

She didn't think. Her body decided before the rest of her caught up, twisting away from the hand closing on her arm, her bag swinging up and catching him hard across the face on pure reflex rather than intent. He swore, staggered back a step. A second man was already round the front of the car, cutting toward her from the other side, and a third coming fast from behind, and for one flat second the street narrowed down to nothing but the three of them and the distance between her and her own front door.

She ran.

Not toward the car, not toward the tea stall where a shopkeeper had already half-risen off his stool at the shouting. Toward her building, the only direction her legs seemed to know. A hand caught her dupatta and she let it go rather than let it slow her, felt the fabric tear loose behind her, kept moving. Someone shouted her name - not kindly, not a name called by someone who knew her, more a word flung to make her flinch and lose half a step - and she didn't flinch, or if she did her legs didn't listen to it.

The street did what streets in this city always did with trouble - it watched. A cyclist swerved wide and kept pedalling. Two boys near the paan shop stopped mid-argument to stare, doing nothing else. Nobody moved to help her, and she didn't waste breath expecting them to; whatever this was, it had made itself look, in the space of a few seconds, exactly ordinary enough that a stranger's instinct was to look away rather than in.

The gate. The stairwell, three at a time, her own ragged breathing louder in her ears than whatever was or wasn't still behind her. She didn't look back to check. Looking back cost time, and time was the one thing she had exactly none of to spend.

Satya's door. Her key skidding once against the lock before finding it, the sound of it turning the single most enormous thing she'd ever heard, and then she was through and shouting his name before the door had even finished swinging.

He was there in a second, out of the kitchen, already reading something wrong in her face before she'd said a word. "What happened-"

"Close it. Close the door."

He did, fast, the deadbolt going home with a sound that landed in her chest like something finally allowed to stop. She was still gasping, one hand pressed flat against the wall to keep herself upright, when she heard footsteps arrive on the landing outside - unhurried now, the running over, whoever they were no longer chasing anything, just standing there, close enough that she could hear one of them breathing hard through his nose on the other side of a door that suddenly felt very thin indeed.

"Manaswini." Satya's hands were on her shoulders, steady, his voice pitched low and level in a way that should have felt calming and instead, distantly, felt like something she didn't have room to examine right now. "Tell me what happened."

"Three men. A car. They tried to-" She couldn't finish it properly, the sentence collapsing somewhere between what had happened and how absurd it sounded said out loud in her own living room. "They tried to take me."

He didn't ask who. He didn't ask why, not yet, not in the two or three seconds it took him to cross to the door and put his eye to the peephole, one hand flat against the wood as though he could feel through it whatever was still standing on the other side.

His face, when he turned back to her, had gone very still.
