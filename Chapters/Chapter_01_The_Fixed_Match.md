# Chapter One: The Fixed Match

"It is decided," Pushpak said. "You'll understand later that we did right by you."

"Decided by whom?" Manaswini was still standing in the doorway, one hand on the frame, as though she hadn't yet let herself all the way into the room. "You didn't ask me anything."

"We are asking you now."

"That isn't asking."

She had known before he said it outright - had known from the word she'd caught through the kitchen wall an hour earlier, *rishta*, and from the way her mother's hands had gone still on the tawa for exactly one roti's turning before starting again. Ranjit uncle's voice, low and careful, on the other side of the wood partition. She hadn't needed to hear the rest.

"There is a boy," Pushpak said now, filling in what she'd already half-known. "Good family, government service. Ranjit uncle has spoken to them already."

"You fixed it," she said. "Without asking me."

"This is settled, Manaswini." He said it again, the second time quieter, more to himself than to her, the way a man repeats a thing he needs to still be true by the time he's finished saying it. His hands, folded in his lap, weren't quite steady. "There are debts owed in this house older than you. I won't be the one who dishonours them."

She didn't understand what he meant by that, and something in his face told her he hadn't meant to say it at all - that it had come out sideways, from somewhere he didn't intend to open in front of her. He didn't explain further. She filed it away without knowing yet that she would.

"You've changed," he said instead, steadier now. "Since Delhi. You talk different, you look at us different. Your mother cries some nights, you know that?"

"Because I have opinions now?"

"Because we don't know who you are becoming."

That, more than anything he could have shouted, was the sentence that stayed with her.

She had gone over it a hundred times in the two years since - the hostel room with the peeling blue paint, three girls to a cot's width of floor space, someone's cousin's copy of a Butler essay passed hand to hand until the pages went soft. Independence. Autonomy. Words that had sounded, the first time she heard them said seriously, like doors opening somewhere she hadn't known was a room. She had taken to them the way people take to a language learned late - loudly, gratefully, without yet knowing where the grammar could trip her.

She hadn't meant to become someone her mother cried over. She had only meant to stop apologising for thinking.

"I'm not marrying a man I've never met," she said now, "because Ranjit uncle thinks it's a good match."

"Your mother married me the same way."

"And are you happy she did?"

It was a cruel question and she knew it the moment it left her mouth. Pushpak didn't answer it. He looked at her the way he might look at a field he no longer recognised the crop in, and said only, "The talks have started. We are not going back on our word now, in front of the whole village."

"Then you'll be going back on it without me."

Sarita, who had been standing in the doorway with a plate she'd forgotten she was holding, said her daughter's name once, quietly, the way you'd call a child back from the edge of something. Manaswini didn't answer that either. She went out past her, into the dark of the courtyard, and stood there until the shape of the mountains against the sky stopped feeling like the only shape she'd ever known.

She didn't decide it that night, not fully. It arrived instead the way weather arrives - already halfway to being certain before she'd admitted to feeling it at all.

Somewhere below the ridge a dog was barking at nothing. The lights in the kitchen window had gone out; her parents had gone to bed believing, she supposed, that a door closed on an argument was the same as a door closed on the matter itself. She stood a while longer with her arms wrapped around herself against the cold, running her thumb along the frayed hem of her dupatta, turning her father's strange sentence over without finding a shape that fit it - *debts older than you* - and thought, not for the first time, but for the first time without flinching from it, that she was going to leave.

Not tomorrow. Not with a scene, not with a slammed door. Quietly, the way she'd learned to do everything since Delhi had taught her that the loudest exits were rarely the ones that actually got you anywhere.

She looked back once at the house - stone and slate, holding its old shape against the hillside the way it had held it for three generations before her - and went inside to sleep, already rehearsing, without quite admitting it to herself, the sound a door makes when it opens without waking anyone at all.
