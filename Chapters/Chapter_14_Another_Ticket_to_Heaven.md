# Chapter Fourteen: Another Ticket to Heaven

Satya switched the television on the way he did most evenings now, without particularly wanting to watch anything, the noise of it more company than the silence would have been since Manaswini had stopped going to Aanchal. She was in the kitchen, only half-listening, when the anchor's voice changed register in that particular way she'd learned, without meaning to, to recognise - the pitch reserved for things that mattered more than whatever had come before them.

She came to the doorway in time to see the photograph fill the screen. Zoya, smiling in something that looked like an old office party picture, the caption beneath her running out details Manaswini's mind refused, for a full three seconds, to arrange into a sentence she could understand. Found in a lift. Mumbai. Foul play suspected.

"No," she said, to no one, to the television, to the fact of it. "No, that's not - I just spoke to her last week."

She didn't remember crossing the room. She was on the sofa with her hand pressed over her mouth, the anchor's voice going on without her, details she couldn't take in about a police investigation and a grieving family in Chembur, and somewhere in the middle of it she became aware that Satya, standing behind her, hadn't said anything at all.

She turned to look at him, needing, without knowing why, to see her own horror reflected back at her from somewhere. It wasn't there. He was watching the screen with an expression she'd never seen on him before - not shock, not the careful blankness he wore when he didn't want her reading him. Something closer to satisfaction, thin and private, the corner of his mouth pulling up in a way that had no business being anywhere near this news.

"Satya."

He looked down at her, and the small, terrible smile didn't leave his face even then. "Another ticket to heaven," he said, almost fond, the way you'd say something about a competitor finally losing a match you'd been rooting against for years.

The room went very still around her.

"What did you just say?"

"Nothing." He reached for the remote, unhurried, and she caught his wrist before he could change the channel, her grip harder than she'd meant it to be.

"You laughed. Zoya is dead and you laughed."

"I didn't laugh."

"You're still smiling."

He looked at her properly then, and whatever had been amused in his face folded itself away, replaced by something flatter, more careful, a man recalculating how much of a conversation he was actually willing to have. "You don't understand what she was."

"She was my friend." Her voice cracked on the word, and she hated that it did, hated that grief was making her sound weaker in this exact moment when she needed to sound like the opposite of weak. "She brought mithai to the office when the flood campaign hit target. She called me every Friday to make me come out dancing. She was a person, Satya, and you're standing there looking pleased that someone strangled her in a lift."

"She was Aanchal." He said it the way he'd said everything two nights ago in this same room - flatly, as though the word itself were an explanation that shouldn't need further defending. "The same as Akhilesh. The same as the men who came for you. You want to grieve her, grieve her. But don't ask me to pretend I'm sorry two more of them are gone."

"Two more of *them*. Listen to yourself."

"I am listening to myself." Something sharper came into his voice now, the first real heat she'd heard from him since the confession, and it frightened her more than his calm ever had. "They aren't innocents, Manaswini. Whatever you think Aanchal is, whatever they let you believe you were doing there - it was never what you thought."

"Then tell me what it actually was." She was standing now, though she didn't remember getting up, her whole body shaking with something that wasn't quite grief anymore and wasn't quite fury either, some third thing she didn't have a name for. "Stop giving me half a sentence every time and then shutting the door on it. If you know something, tell me. All of it."

He looked at her for a long moment, something working behind his eyes that she couldn't read, and for a second she thought - hoped - that he actually would.

"Not tonight," he said instead, and turned the television off himself, leaving her standing in a room gone suddenly and completely silent, with a dead friend's face still burning behind her eyes and a sentence he wouldn't finish hanging in the air between them.
