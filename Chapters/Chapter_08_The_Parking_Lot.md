# Chapter Eight: The Parking Lot

The market car park filled up the way it did every evening, in loose unhurried rows, nobody parking in a straight line because nobody ever had to.

Devraj had been running the paan stall at the gate for eleven years and knew most of the regular faces without needing their names - the woman who bought two Marlboros and never a whole pack, the security guard who came off shift at seven and always wanted his paan sweet, no tobacco. He was folding one now, unhurried, listening with half an ear to the guard's complaint about his knees, when a scooter went past a little too fast and neither of them thought anything of it at all.

Twelve rows back, a woman named Kavita was buckling her son into the back seat of a hatchback that had taken her husband four years to save up for, telling the boy for the third time to stop kicking the seat in front of him, her mind already three streets ahead on the evening's shopping list. Near the ramp, two college boys argued cheerfully over whose turn it was to pay for petrol. A guard dozed lightly in a plastic chair by the boom barrier, cap pulled low, in no hurry to notice the white sedan that had been parked, unremarked, in the same corner spot since mid-afternoon.

Nobody had reason to look at the sedan twice. That was, in the end, the whole of the plan.

The driver was still inside it when it went. Nobody agreed afterward on what he looked like, or whether there'd been anything to see at all in that last handful of seconds - most of the accounts collected in the following days contradicted each other on every detail except one, repeated by nearly everyone who survived close enough to have an opinion: that there had been no warning in it. No shout. No last look around. Just an ordinary parked car among many, and then, without any interval a human mind could properly register as a gap, not a car at all.

The sound arrived before the understanding of it did. A single flat concussion that didn't so much fill the ears as empty them, followed by a half-second of nothing - no birds, no traffic, no Devraj's paan-folding hands - before the world came rushing back in fragments too loud to sort into any order. Glass. Somewhere, a car alarm looping uselessly into the smoke. Kavita, thrown against her own steering wheel hard enough to crack it, twisted round in her seat before the ringing in her ears had even cleared, both hands already reaching for the back seat, for her son, for the small furious howl of a boy who was, by the mercy of twelve rows' distance, only frightened.

Fifteen people did not get up again. Thirty more would spend that night and many after it in hospital corridors, counting what they'd kept and what they hadn't. Devraj found himself on the ground without remembering falling, a ringing in one ear that would never fully leave him, his paan-tray somewhere in the smoke he didn't have it in him yet to go looking for. The security guard who liked his paan sweet was already on his feet, moving toward the wreckage on legs that hadn't yet caught up with what his eyes were reporting, shouting a name that belonged to no one anyone could account for.

Sirens arrived the way sirens always do - too slow for the people already past helping, fast enough that the ones still moving would remember, for years, the particular colour the lights threw across broken glass. A woman in a torn dupatta sat very still against a pillar, not screaming, not weeping, simply staring at her own hands as though she'd misplaced something in them and couldn't work out what. Somewhere a television van's satellite dish was already unfolding, three streets away, the anchor's voice - the same even, practised register that had read out a warning about Mumbai and Hyderabad on screens across the city not two weeks before - now finding, without much adjustment needed to the tone, a new city to name instead.

By the time the first stretcher was loaded, the smoke had thinned enough to see the full shape of what the car park had been an hour ago and would never entirely be again - the neat unhurried rows, the paan stall, the boom barrier still standing, absurdly, exactly where it always had. None of the raids two weeks earlier had come anywhere near this market. Nobody had thought to. It hadn't been on anybody's list.

By morning, forensic teams would have a name for the man who'd sat behind that wheel - Dr. Zakaruddin, though the how and why of him would take far longer to surface than the fact of him - and a substance to write into their reports: ammonium nitrate, not the RDX investigators had half-expected, mixed and readied somewhere far from any market by hands that would never once have to look at what fifteen faces looked like afterward.

Two constables, waiting by the tape for someone to tell them they could finally go home, turned it over between themselves the quiet, unhurried way policemen at a scene often did.

"A doctor," one of them said, still turning the word over as though it didn't fit the rest of the sentence. "MBBS. Was practising, somewhere in UP, before all this."

"So?"

"So how does a man who's taken an oath to save people end up doing this."

The other one shrugged, the gesture of someone who'd stopped expecting the job to hand him an answer to that particular question years ago. "Education teaches a man skill," he said. "Nobody sits him down separately and teaches him right from wrong. That part he's meant to arrive with, or find for himself. Some men don't."

It was, by morning, on every list there was.
