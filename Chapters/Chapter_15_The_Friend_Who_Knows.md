# Chapter Fifteen: The Friend Who Knows

Satya called Vishesh a little after midnight, once he was certain Manaswini had finally fallen asleep, and said only, "I need to see you tonight," in a voice flat enough that Vishesh didn't ask why before agreeing.

They met in Vishesh's car, parked two streets from the flat, the engine off and the windows cracked against the night's leftover heat. It felt, Satya thought, absurdly like something out of the very films he'd have mocked a year ago - two men talking in a parked car at one in the morning - except that nothing about how he felt sitting in it was absurd at all.

"She wants the whole story," Satya said. "Tonight, after the news, she asked me straight out. I can't keep giving her half of it."

"Then give her the whole of it." Vishesh said it evenly, though something behind his eyes suggested he understood exactly what that would cost. "You've been carrying this alone in front of her. That was never the plan."

"Tell me again. From the start. I want to be sure I get it right when I tell her."

Vishesh exhaled, and began.

The day after the blast, before Satya had even finished processing that fifteen people were dead in a car park three kilometres from his office, Vishesh had come to him with something he'd been sitting on for months and finally had confirmation of: Aanchal's books didn't add up, hadn't for years, and the money that went missing followed a route that ended, eventually, in the hands of the men who'd prepared the ammonium nitrate that killed them - not the whole operation, just that one link in it, but a real one, traceable if you knew where to look. An informant of Vishesh's - a junior clerk at one of Aanchal's partner banks, feeding him transaction patterns on the quiet - had gone silent three months earlier, right around the time he'd started asking too many questions about a particular disbursement chain. Vishesh had never been allowed to reopen that case through the proper channels. Nobody above him had wanted to touch an NGO with Aanchal's public profile on the strength of one missing clerk and a hunch.

The blast changed that math for him personally, if not officially. "I had confirmation," Vishesh said now, "and no clean way to use it. Akhilesh and Zoya weren't the whole chain - Revanth runs deeper than either of them, and above him, someone richer still, funding it all. But Akhilesh and Zoya were the ones who'd physically carried money to hands that built bombs. That much I could prove, to myself if to no one else."

"And Manaswini was standing in the middle of it," Satya said. "Working for them. Trusting them."

"That's what made it your problem and not just mine." Vishesh turned to look at him properly. "I could have sat on what I knew, kept building a case the slow way, and let two more years pass while your girlfriend kept raising them donation money. Or we could stop being patient."

Satya remembered, with a clarity that hadn't dulled in the weeks since, exactly how little debate that choice had actually needed. "We agreed to eliminate them ourselves."

"We agreed you would eliminate them," Vishesh corrected, without apology. "I provided the means, and I covered what needed covering afterward. You did the rest. I'm not going to sit here and pretend my hands are cleaner than yours just because I didn't hold the needle."

It was the closest either of them had come, in the weeks since, to naming exactly what they'd done to each other's conscience. Satya let it sit a moment before asking the question he'd been circling since Zoya's face had filled that television screen. "And Zoya. That wasn't me."

"No." For the first time, something in Vishesh's voice thinned, careful in a way it hadn't been for Akhilesh. "Her name is Ritika. A sub-inspector, my own division, though not someone I'd worked with closely before this. Her mother was on that list of fifteen. I went to her three days after the blast, when I still didn't know if I could trust you to see this through cleanly, and I asked her a question I had no business asking a fellow officer - whether she'd want to be the hand that closed one small part of the account, off the books, no trail back to either of us."

"She said yes."

"She said yes before I'd finished the sentence." Vishesh looked out through the windscreen at nothing in particular. "I'm not proud of how easy that was. I knew what her mother's death would make her willing to do before I ever asked her. That's not nothing, Satya. I used a grieving woman's grief as a tool, the same as you used a needle. Neither of us gets to feel clean about our half of this."

Satya sat with that a long moment, the city outside gone quiet in the particular way it only went past one in the morning, and thought about Manaswini asleep three streets away, still believing, until tomorrow, that the man beside her had acted alone.

"I have to tell her some of this," he said finally. "Not all of it. She doesn't need Ritika's name, doesn't need to know you exist in this at all beyond a friend who happened to have a badge."

"That's your call to make." Vishesh started the engine. "Just be sure, when you make it, that you're protecting her and not just yourself."

Satya didn't answer that. He wasn't certain, walking the two quiet streets back in the dark a few minutes later, that he entirely trusted his own answer either way.
