# Chapter Seventeen: The Locked Door

Satya woke before her, the way he usually did, and lay a while watching Manaswini sleep with an unease that hadn't left him since the previous night's silence had finally tipped over into something like exhausted sleep for both of them. She hadn't taken back a word of it. She was going to Aanchal today, whatever he said, whatever he begged, and some cold, practical part of him - the same part that had once weighed a needle against a corridor and found the weighing easy - had already begun working out what he was actually willing to do about that.

He didn't wake her to argue it again. There was nothing left in the argument that hadn't already been said, and some instinct told him a second round of it this morning would only harden her further. Instead he dressed quietly, wrote nothing, left no note, and locked the door from the outside behind him with a decision he knew, even as he made it, he wouldn't be able to defend to her later.

It wasn't really about keeping her in. He told himself that on the stairs, and mostly believed it. It was about buying an hour - one hour in which she couldn't walk out that door toward a building full of people who thought she was a leak worth interrogating, one hour in which he could get to Vishesh, find out if there was any way to make Aanchal stand down before she put herself directly in front of them. He told himself it was protection. He didn't examine too closely how much it also looked, from any angle but his own, like something considerably uglier than that.

He stood on the landing a moment with the key still warm in his palm, half-tempted to go back in and simply wake her, tell her honestly what he was about to do and why, and let her be as furious about it as she liked. He didn't. Some part of him had learned, across the last month, that asking her permission for the things that frightened him most was a habit he'd stopped trusting himself to keep - not because she'd ever refused him anything that mattered, but because he no longer entirely trusted his own judgment about which decisions were still his alone to make.

Vishesh answered on the second ring, already awake, already sounding like a man who'd expected this call eventually. "She's still going," Satya said, without preamble.

"Today?"

"Today. I've bought myself an hour. I don't know what to do with it."

They talked in circles for most of that hour - whether Vishesh could quietly flag Aanchal's office for a routine visit that would keep Revanth's people occupied elsewhere, whether there was any version of a warning that could reach Manaswini without her understanding exactly how far Vishesh's involvement in all of this actually ran. Nothing they landed on felt like enough. Satya kept checking his watch, some part of him already counting down to the moment he'd have to go back and unlock that door and face what he'd done, and some other part of him, quieter and more frightened than he wanted to admit even to Vishesh, simply wanting to buy a second hour, and then a third, for as long as it took to make her safe by force if he couldn't manage it any other way.

In the end he left Vishesh mid-sentence, an unfinished plan hanging between them, because the hour had run out and some instinct he trusted more than either of their reasoning told him to get back to the flat now rather than later.

He took the stairs two at a time. The corridor outside their door was empty, ordinary, nothing in it that should have made his stomach drop the way it did the moment he saw the lock.

It was open. Not forced - no splintered frame, no obvious violence to the wood, just the deadbolt sitting where he'd left it and the door itself standing a few careless inches ajar, as though whoever had left it that way hadn't particularly cared whether anyone noticed.

"Manaswini."

No answer. He pushed the door open the rest of the way and stood in the doorway of a flat gone completely, wrongly silent - her bag still on the hook by the door, her phone still charging on the kitchen counter, a cup of tea gone cold and untouched on the table exactly where he'd left it that morning.

She was gone.
