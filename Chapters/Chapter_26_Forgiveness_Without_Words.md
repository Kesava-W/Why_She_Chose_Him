# Chapter Twenty-Six: Forgiveness Without Words

Satya signalled Vishesh from the doorway, a small motion she almost missed, and Vishesh was gone only a few minutes before he returned with two people Manaswini hadn't seen in nearly a year, both of them stopping dead in the doorway as though the sight of her - bandaged, thinner than they remembered, tubes still taped into the back of one hand - had cost them the last of whatever composure they'd managed to hold together on the drive here.

Vishesh didn't follow them in. He stopped in the doorway just long enough to catch Satya's eye, a small nod passing between the two men that Manaswini was too full of her own parents' arrival to notice, and then he was gone, leaving the four of them alone with whatever this hour was going to become.

"Manaswini." Her mother said it first, a whisper more than a word, already crossing the room before her father had fully processed that he was allowed to move.

"Ma."

Sarita took her hand carefully, mindful of the tubes, and pressed it between both of hers the way she used to when Manaswini was small and running a fever, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all, the better part of a year and everything that had happened inside it sitting silently between their joined hands.

Pushpak stood a step back, his face doing something complicated that she didn't have the strength yet to fully read - relief, certainly, but underneath it something else, older and harder to name, the same quality she remembered from a front room almost a year ago and a sentence he'd never finished explaining. *There are debts owed in this house older than you.* He didn't say anything like that now. He only looked at her, and looked, for one unguarded second, almost as frightened by having nearly lost her as by whatever it was he still hadn't told her, and then the moment passed and he was simply her father again, standing at the foot of a hospital bed, saying nothing at all.

"I acted foolishly," Manaswini said, once she trusted her voice enough to use it. "I didn't think about what leaving would cost either of you. I didn't think about anything except what I wanted, and I let that be enough for a very long time." She looked between them, her mother's hand still wrapped around hers, her father still standing at the careful distance he seemed unable to close. "I'm asking you to forgive me. Not for falling in love, not for wanting something different from what you wanted for me - for the way I did it. For the years I let you believe I hated you, when I didn't. I never did."

Sarita's eyes filled, and she pressed Manaswini's hand tighter without saying anything at all.

Pushpak didn't speak either. He came forward at last, slowly, and sat on the edge of the bed the way he hadn't dared to a minute earlier, and reached out to touch her bandaged head with a gentleness that undid something in her more completely than any word could have. He wasn't smiling. He wasn't, she understood, even close to being at peace with everything that had happened - the running away, the marriage she'd refused, the better part of a year's silence, the phone call two nights ago that had told him his daughter had nearly died in a place he still didn't fully understand. None of that was resolved. None of it was going to be resolved tonight, or perhaps for a long time after tonight.

But he was here. He had come the moment Vishesh called. And that, she understood, watching his hand rest against her hair, was its own kind of answer, offered in the only language her father had ever been entirely fluent in.

He looked past her, finally, to where Satya stood near the window, and something passed between the two men that Manaswini couldn't fully read - her father's face unreadable, weighing something, before he said, in a voice rough with everything he wasn't saying out loud, "Take care of her."

"I will," Satya said. "I promise you that."

Pushpak nodded once, and turned back to his daughter, and said nothing more about forgiveness at all - not that night, and not, Manaswini would come to understand over the months that followed, in any of the words either of them would ever quite manage to find for it. It didn't need saying. It was sitting there already, in the weight of his hand against her hair, in the better part of a year's silence he'd just crossed a country to end.
