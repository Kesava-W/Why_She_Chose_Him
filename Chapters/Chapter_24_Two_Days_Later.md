# Chapter Twenty-Four: Two Days Later

Two days later, Manaswini woke.

The room came to her in careful pieces, the way the factory had left her - a white ceiling first, unfamiliar and blessedly still. The soft insistent beep of a monitor somewhere to her left. The particular antiseptic smell that told her, before anything else did, that she was somewhere safe even before she understood where safe actually was. Her head throbbed under a bandage she could feel without seeing, and her wrists, when she finally found the strength to lift one, carried raw red lines that would, she understood distantly, take weeks to properly fade.

"She's awake."

Satya's voice, close, and then his face above hers, drawn and unshaven in a way she'd never once seen him allow himself to look. Behind him, a step back, Vishesh stood with his hands in his pockets, watching with an expression she couldn't read and didn't, in that moment, have the strength to try reading.

"Where-"

"Hospital. Delhi. You're safe." Satya's hand found hers, gripped it hard enough that she felt it through the fog still clouding most of her thinking. "You've been out two days. The doctors say you're going to be fine. Physically, you're going to be fine."

Vishesh cleared his throat, an awkward, careful sound. "Good to see you awake, Manaswini." It was the first thing he'd ever said to her that wasn't a joke made at Satya's expense, and something in its plainness made her want to ask him a hundred questions she didn't yet have the strength to form. She only nodded at him, and he nodded back, and neither of them tried for more than that.

She let that settle, piece by piece - the factory, the gunfire, the stranger shouting for his father, the last clear memory of gentle hands at the rope before everything else went dark. "My parents. Are they-"

"Safe. Untouched. I promise you that." Something in his face, saying it, didn't sit quite right against the plainness of the words, though she was too exhausted to chase the feeling any further than noticing it. "You don't need to worry about them right now."

She nodded, something in her chest loosening at that, and then another fragment surfaced, slower and heavier than the others - a hand gone from her shoulder, a shape on the ground she'd never let herself look at properly. "Aniket. Is he-"

Satya's face answered before his voice managed to. "He didn't survive it. I'm sorry, Manaswini."

She'd known, she realised, somewhere beneath the asking - had known since the factory floor, since the exact moment his grip had simply stopped being there and never come back. Hearing it said aloud didn't break anything freshly open in her. It only settled, quietly and completely, something that had already been waiting most of two days to be settled.

She nodded once, and let her eyes close again, not from unconsciousness this time but from something closer to relief finally being allowed to arrive alongside the grief. When she opened them again some indeterminate time later - minutes, she thought, though it might have been longer - Vishesh had gone, and Satya was sitting in the chair beside her bed with his hands folded, watching her with an expression that had changed into something she didn't recognise on him at all.

"I need to tell you something," he said.

"Okay."

"I'm breaking up with you."

The words arrived with no warning attached to them, no softening, nothing in his tone that had prepared her for the shape of what he was actually saying. For a moment she genuinely thought she'd misheard him, some lingering fog from the sedation twisting his sentence into something it couldn't possibly have meant.

"What?"

"I'm ending this. Us." He said it steadily, looking at her rather than away, which somehow made it worse, not better - a man delivering something he'd decided rather than something that had simply happened to him. "I think it's better if I go, once you're discharged. I'll make sure you're settled somewhere safe first. But I can't keep doing this."

"Satya, I just woke up. I was tied to a chair two days ago. I don't understand what's happening right now."

"I know." Something in his face flickered, the first crack she'd seen in whatever composure he'd walked into the room with. "I know the timing is cruel. I'm telling you anyway, because I don't think I'll manage to say it if I wait until you're stronger and can talk me out of it."

She stared at him, the raw wrists, the ache in her skull, the whole impossible weight of the last two weeks pressing down on her all at once, and found that beneath the shock there was, unexpectedly, no anger at all - only a kind of desperate, wordless refusal, her hand tightening around his before she'd decided to move it.

"Don't," she said. "Please. Whatever this is, whatever's made you decide this - don't leave. Not now. Not like this."

"You were right. About Aanchal, about all of it." She pulled his hand to her chest and held it there with both of hers, and did not let go. "You're the one thing in this that was never fake."

He didn't pull his hand away. He sat there, letting her hold it, his jaw tight and his eyes fixed somewhere past her shoulder, and said nothing at all - not agreement, not refusal, just a long, terrible silence that told her, more clearly than any answer could have, that he hadn't yet finished deciding, and that whatever he decided next was going to cost one of them everything.
