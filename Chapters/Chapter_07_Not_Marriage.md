# Chapter Seven: Not Marriage

He asked her on an ordinary Tuesday, which she would think, afterward, was exactly like him - no restaurant booked months in advance, no ring produced from some rehearsed pocket, just the two of them clearing plates after a dinner he'd cooked slightly too much of, and Satya saying, in the same even voice he might have used to ask whether she wanted the last of the rice, "Marry me."

She put the plate down before she answered, because her hands had gone briefly unreliable.

"Say that again."

"Marry me." He wasn't smiling, not exactly, but something in his face had gone unusually open, undefended in a way she'd learned by now to notice precisely because it happened so rarely. "I've thought about it properly. I'm not asking because it's the next thing people do."

She loved him. She knew that with a clarity that made what came next harder rather than easier. "I can't."

"Can't, or won't?"

"Both, maybe. I don't know how to explain it so it doesn't sound like I'm choosing something over you."

"Try."

She sat down across from him instead of beside him, needing, she realised, the small distance of a table between them to say this properly. "I left my parents' house because a marriage was decided for me without my say in it. I know this is different - I know you're asking, not fixing something behind my back. But the shape of it is still the same shape. A husband. A house that becomes ours on paper in a way that somehow, always, ends up meaning mine less than his. I've watched it happen to every married woman I grew up around, including my own mother. I'm not ready to find out whether it would happen to me too."

"I'm not your father."

"I know that. I do know that." She reached across and took his hand, because she needed him to understand this wasn't a verdict on him. "This isn't about you failing some test. It's that I only just learned how to be someone who makes her own decisions, and marriage feels like handing the pen back to someone else, even someone I trust with it."

He didn't argue the way she'd half-braced for. He sat with it, quiet long enough that she started, absurdly, to wonder if she'd misjudged how badly this would land, and then he said, evenly, "Then don't marry me."

"Satya-"

"I mean it. Not as a punishment, not as me sulking off to prove a point." He turned her hand over in his, tracing an idle line across her palm, buying himself, she suspected, a few more seconds to arrive at whatever he was arriving at. "Move in with me properly. Not the toothbrush-and-three-nights arrangement we've already basically got. All the way in. If marriage is the wrong shape for you right now, we'll just build a different one."

"You'd be all right with that? Really?"

A pause, brief, that she filed away without being able to name exactly what it contained - something that looked, for half a second, like a man setting a private disappointment down somewhere out of sight rather than carrying it into the room with them. "I'd rather have you on your terms," he said, "than not have you on mine."

It wasn't a yes offered freely, she understood that even as she said yes to it herself, laughing, crying a little, kissing him hard enough to knock the last of the dinner plates dangerously close to the table's edge. It was a man conceding more than he was saying out loud, choosing to keep her rather than win the argument, and some quieter part of her - the part that had learned, growing up, to read exactly how much a silence in a Kumaoni household could be carrying - understood that this particular yes had cost him something he wasn't going to itemise for her tonight, or possibly ever.

His phone rang before either of them had properly recovered. He glanced at the screen, and something in his shoulders eased, ordinary again. "Vishesh," he said, by way of explanation, already reaching for it. "He'll want to hear this."

The call was short, mostly Satya laughing at whatever teasing was coming down the line, agreeing to meet for a drink to "make it official," and somewhere in the middle of it his voice dropped half a register, tired in a way that had nothing to do with the good news he'd just delivered. "Yeah, still nothing solid on that NGO thing," he said, half turned away from her, rubbing at his eyes with his free hand. "Some week you're going to have to let that one go, yaar."

He hung up, phone already forgotten on the counter before it had properly landed, and reached for her again as though the call had been nothing more than the ordinary interruption it looked like.

"Where were we," he said, and she showed him, and the rest of the evening went on exactly as good evenings do - too full of better things to leave room for anything else.
