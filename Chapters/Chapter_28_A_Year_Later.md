# Chapter Twenty-Eight: A Year Later

A year on, the house still stood stone and slate against the hillside the way it had held its shape for three generations before her, and Manaswini had learned, slowly, to see it as something other than a thing she'd once needed to escape.

The school she'd started ran out of two rented rooms above the old grain store, funded on a shoestring by Satya's salary and whatever small grants she could coax out of city foundations. Twenty-three girls, this term, learning English and arithmetic and, more than either, the particular confidence of being taken seriously by an adult who'd once sat exactly where they were sitting. She taught three mornings a week herself, and had stopped needing anyone's permission to call that work important.

Her mother had asked her, only once, gently, whether she resented coming back to the very place she'd run from in the dark. Manaswini had thought before answering. "I didn't come back to the place," she'd said. "I came back to build something different inside it. That's not the same as losing."

Their son was a few months old now, still too young to do much besides sleep and startle and grip a finger with a surprising strength - already a fixture of the school's two rooms, passed hand to hand between the older girls, none of them willing to admit how much they fought over whose turn it was to hold him.

It was on one such evening - the school day finished, dinner cleared, her mother's chareu catching the last of the light through the kitchen window - that she found her father alone in the courtyard, and asked him, finally, the question she'd carried unanswered for almost two years.

"You said something once. Before I left. About debts older than me."

Pushpak was quiet a long moment, turning something over that she understood, watching him, had cost him more than the silence let on. "Your grandfather," he said finally. "Before you were born - a bad harvest, three years running, and Ranjit's father lent us what kept this house standing. No interest, no paper signed, just his word and mine. I always meant to repay it properly, in a way that mattered to both families." Something apologetic crossed his face. "The match wasn't only fear for you, Manaswini. It was also an old man trying to close an old account before it outlived him. I should have told you that plainly, instead of half a sentence that frightened you more than the truth would have."

She sat with that a while, watching the light go gold and then grey over the terraces below. It wasn't sinister, in the end. It was only what most things in this village turned out to be, once you asked properly instead of running from the asking - old, human, and considerably less frightening once it was finally said aloud.

That evening, in the traditional dress she'd taken, over the year, to wearing without any of the old resentment it had once carried for her, she sat with Satya on the low wall outside their own small house, their son settled warm and heavy in the crook of her arm, and asked him the question she'd never quite found the right moment for in the year since the hospital.

"How did you actually save them? My parents. That night at the factory - you said they were safe, but you never told me how."

Satya was quiet a moment, the way he still sometimes went quiet before something he'd decided, long ago, he needed to say properly rather than quickly. "I went to them myself," he said. "The day before, once Vishesh and I understood what Aanchal's backer was demanding. I told your father there was danger, real danger, and that I needed to move them somewhere safe for a few days without anyone at Aanchal knowing they'd gone. He didn't ask many questions. I think, by then, he trusted that I wouldn't lie to him about something like that."

"And the car. The one Revanth fired on."

"Vishesh's doing, not mine." He looked out over the terraces with her, the same view she'd once walked away from in the dark. "He found out who the backer was days before the exchange - a man named Aryan Malhotra. Vishesh had his own people kidnap Malhotra's parents, quietly, and put them exactly where yours were meant to be taken from. Malhotra never knew the difference until it was already too late to matter. Revanth fired on a car believing it held your parents. It never did."

She sat with the shape of it a long moment - the year of not knowing, the stranger's voice shouting for his father in a factory she still dreamed about some nights, the quiet machinery two men had built around her without her ever seeing it run. "You could have told me all of this a year ago."

"I could have." He took her hand, the same steady grip she'd first trusted in a bar a year and a half earlier now. "I've been trying to learn to tell you things properly, instead of deciding for you what you're strong enough to carry. I'm still learning it. I'll probably still be learning it a decade from now."

She looked at him a long moment - at the man who had killed for her, lied to her, tested her cruelly and then held her hand through every consequence of all three - and found, beneath everything the last two years had cost her, something that had finally, quietly, stopped needing to be certain of him in order to choose him anyway.

"Good," she said. "Keep learning it. I'm not going anywhere."

Satya rested a finger against their son's small closed hand, and the baby's fingers found it and gripped, blind and certain, the way they gripped everything.

Below them, the terraces held the last of the light a moment longer, and then let it go, gently, the way the mountains always did.
