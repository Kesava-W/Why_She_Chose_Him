# Chapter Nine: The Sting

Satya drove home with both hands too tight on the wheel, the radio off, the city sliding past the windscreen in a way he wasn't, if he was honest with himself, actually watching. He'd been like this since the afternoon - some private calculation running behind his eyes that he hadn't shared with her that morning and wasn't going to share with her tonight either, not before it was finished. He caught himself, twice, at red lights he'd stopped for without remembering the amber. Both times he told himself it was tiredness. Neither time did he believe it.

Manaswini, twenty minutes ahead of him across the city, wasn't thinking about Satya at all. She was thinking about the day's last stretch of donor emails, and about Akhilesh, walking beside her from the auto stand with the particular unhurried patience he always had for her when the others had already peeled off toward their own trains, telling her some long, mostly pointless story about a landlord dispute two floors below his own flat.

"You didn't have to walk me all the way up," she said, at the building's entrance.

"I know." He shrugged, easy, the gesture of a man for whom the walk had never really been about obligation. "Ghar tak chhod doon, phir jaata hoon. Force of habit."

They rode the lift together, talking about nothing in particular - the landlord story finishing somewhere around the fourth floor, replaced by his opinion on whether the Bundelkhand campaign should run through the monsoon or wait for it to pass, an opinion she half-listened to while fishing her keys out of her bag before the doors had even opened. He stepped out with her onto the landing, said something about seeing her tomorrow, and had already half-turned to go back down when the lift beside them chimed and opened a second time.

Satya came out of it fast, faster than his usual unhurried walk, still wearing whatever it was that had followed him the whole drive home. He and Akhilesh crossed each other in the narrow stretch of landing between the two lifts - close, the kind of close a corridor forces on two men going opposite ways, a half-second of nothing but proximity.

Manaswini, key already in the lock, glanced back at the small commotion of it more out of habit than interest. She caught the two of them passing - Satya's shoulder dipping slightly as he moved past, Akhilesh's hand coming up briefly to his own side, the kind of small unconscious gesture a man makes adjusting a bag strap or scratching at nothing - and then the door was open under her hand and she was already stepping through it, already saying something to Satya about how his evening had been, already forgetting, before the sentence was finished, that she'd seen anything at all.

"Bye, Akhilesh," she called back, not quite turning.

"Bye," he said, already three steps toward the stairwell, in no apparent hurry, rubbing absently at his hip the way you'd rub away a mosquito bite you couldn't be bothered to properly locate.

Satya didn't watch him go. He came through the door behind her, kissed the top of her head in the ordinary way he always did, and asked how her day had been in a voice that had, somewhere in the last ninety seconds, found its way back to entirely even.

"Long," she said. "Akhilesh walked me up, as usual. You look tired."

"I am tired." He set his keys down with more care than keys usually required, a small precise gesture she noticed and didn't think anything of. "Bad day at work. Nothing worth talking about."

She let it go, the way you let go of a hundred small things a day in a relationship that had stopped requiring an explanation for every mood. She went to change out of her work clothes; he went to the kitchen and stood there a while, longer than making tea should have taken, his hands flat on the counter and his head down, the posture of a man either praying or waiting for something in himself to finish happening.

By the time she came back out he had two cups made, and whatever had been sitting behind his eyes on the drive home had folded itself away somewhere she couldn't see, replaced by the ordinary tiredness of an ordinary evening. They ate dinner in front of the television. He asked about the Bundelkhand campaign with real interest, laughed at the right moments, went to bed at the usual time and slept, as far as she could tell, without any trouble at all.

She thought nothing more of the corridor. There had been nothing in it to think about - two men passing each other on a landing, the way a hundred pairs of men pass each other on a hundred landings every evening in a city of this size, none of it worth the effort of a second glance.

It would be the very next day that Akhilesh failed to turn up at Aanchal at all.
