# Chapter Ten: Missing

The desk was empty when she came in, which wasn't in itself unusual - Akhilesh kept his own hours more than most, in and out on field visits nobody else tracked closely - but by eleven, when he still hadn't answered two calls and one message from Revanth's own phone, the empty desk had stopped looking like an ordinary Tuesday and started looking like something people were choosing not to say out loud yet.

"His phone's switched off," Zoya said, dropping into the chair across from Manaswini's with the particular restlessness of someone who wanted a reason to talk rather than actual news to deliver. "Not just unanswered. Off. His wife's called twice already."

"Maybe he's stuck somewhere. Bad reception, out near the districts."

"Maybe." Zoya didn't sound convinced, and didn't try very hard to sound it either.

Manaswini felt the flicker of it - a small, specific unease, the kind you get about a colleague rather than a friend, present enough to notice and not quite large enough to act on - and then the phone on her own desk rang, a donor from Mumbai wanting confirmation on a disbursement timeline, and the unease did what unease usually did in the middle of a working day: it waited.

Aniket found her an hour later, refilling her tea without being asked the way he always did, and sat on the edge of her desk with the particular gentleness he saved for moments he'd decided required it. "You've heard about Akhilesh."

"Zoya told me. Do you know anything?"

"Nothing solid. Revanth's making calls." He studied her a moment, something searching in it that she read, at the time, as simple concern. "You're all right? You seem shaken."

"I'm fine. It's probably nothing."

"It's probably nothing," he agreed, and the way he said it - warm, certain, entirely on her side - was exactly the reassurance she'd wanted without knowing she wanted it, and she let herself be steadied by it the way she let herself be steadied by most things Aniket said to her.

She stayed the whole day. There was a donor call she couldn't reasonably push to tomorrow, a report due by evening that three other people were depending on, and some stubborn, practical part of her found it easier to sit through the ordinary rhythm of the office than to go home to an empty flat and sit with the unease properly. She wrote her emails. She took her calls. She noticed, without quite letting herself notice fully, that the room's usual noise - Zoya's running commentary, the printer's complaints, someone's radio playing low near the pantry - had thinned out over the course of the afternoon into something quieter than a Tuesday should have been.

At some point past lunch she caught herself thinking of the flood-relief campaign, the mithai in the stairwell, Zoya's arm slung warm over her shoulder announcing to the room that they'd found the best feminist in Delhi - and then of Akhilesh, unremarkable at the edges of that same memory, present the way furniture is present, easy to overlook and, she realised now, easy to miss properly only once he was gone. She didn't know him well enough to grieve him. She knew him well enough that his absence sat wrong in the room regardless.

It was near six, packing her bag to leave, that she felt it properly for the first time - not anything said, nothing she could have pointed to and named. Revanth, in the doorway of his office, in conversation with two of the district coordinators she barely knew by name, all three of them going quiet the moment she passed, in a way that felt less like discretion and more like a conversation deliberately paused for her benefit. She caught the tail end of a look between them, something sharp and appraising that didn't belong to grief over a missing colleague, and told herself, walking past it, that she was reading too much into a hard day.

She didn't see the second look, the one that found her back once she'd cleared the doorway and started down the stairs - three faces turning to watch her go, none of them warm, none of them anything close to the ordinary sympathy a departing colleague might expect on a day like this one. It lasted only as long as it took her to reach the landing below. By the time she stepped out into the evening traffic, phone already out, texting Satya that she was on her way home, the look had folded itself back into whatever the office needed it to look like by morning, and no one on that staircase would have told her, if she'd asked, that anything at all had happened behind her at six o'clock that evening.

She hadn't asked. There had been, as far as she knew, nothing to ask about.
