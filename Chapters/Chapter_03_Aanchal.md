# Chapter Three: Aanchal

The Aanchal Group's office sat above a stationery shop in a lane behind Hauz Khas, up a staircase that smelled permanently of incense and photocopier toner, and Manaswini almost walked past it twice before she found the right door. She'd heard the name from Ipsita, a hostel-mate two years ahead of her who was letting her sleep on a mattress in her kitchen until she found her feet, and who'd said, over reheated dal the night before, "They take on English-speakers for the donor calls. Foreign funders like hearing it done properly." It hadn't sounded like a calling. It had sounded, more usefully, like a job.

Revanth interviewed her himself, which she took at the time for a good sign rather than a habit worth questioning. He was younger than she'd expected an NGO chief to be, warm in a practised way that didn't yet strike her as practised, and he asked her to read a paragraph aloud from a grant proposal - something about girls' literacy in Bundelkhand - and watched her the whole way through with an attention she mistook, not unreasonably, for genuine interest in how she sounded.

"You have the voice for this," he said, when she'd finished. "Confident. Educated. Exactly what a donor wants to hear when they're deciding whether their money is safe."

She started the following Monday.

The work suited her better than she'd expected work to suit anyone. Aanchal ran on donations - urban CSR money mostly, some foreign grants, funnelled into programmes for poor and needy women across half a dozen states, or so the brochure said, and Manaswini's job was to be the voice that made the funnelling sound worth doing. She wrote appeal letters that made strangers reach for their chequebooks. She got on calls with donors twice her age and didn't let her own age show in her voice. Within six weeks she'd been handed her first real campaign - flood relief for women's shelters in the eastern districts - and within six weeks after that, she'd raised more than the target, comfortably, the kind of number that got printed on a poster in the office stairwell with her name under it in a font slightly too large for how new she was.

She met the others in pieces, the way you meet colleagues rather than characters in a story - not all at once, not with any sense of who would matter later. Zoya she liked immediately, an easy, funny woman a few years older who ran the Mumbai-facing accounts and made the long donor calls bearable with a running commentary afterward that nobody else could get away with. Akhilesh she saw less of, a bald, quiet man in his late thirties who dealt with logistics and seemed to exist mostly at the edges of other people's meetings, present but unremarked-on, the way furniture is present. And there was Aniket.

Aniket found her a desk on her second day when nobody else had thought to, brought her tea without being asked on a morning she was too nervous about a call to have asked for it herself, and was, within a fortnight, the person at Aanchal she'd have named first if anyone had asked her who she trusted there. He ran field coordination - liaised with the district programmes, signed off on disbursements before they went out, the unglamorous administrative centre that made the glamorous fundraising mean anything at all. He had a habit of remembering small things she'd mentioned only once - her mother's name, the fact that she took her tea without sugar - and repeating them back to her weeks later in a way that felt, at the time, like being seen rather than managed.

"You're wasted on donor calls," he told her once, only half joking. "You should be running programmes, not just paying for them."

She laughed it off. She didn't, in that moment, disbelieve a word of it.

It was Zoya, in the end, who said the sentence that stayed with her longest, on the afternoon the flood-relief numbers came in higher than anyone had projected. The whole floor had gathered loosely around Manaswini's desk, someone had produced a box of mithai from somewhere, and Zoya, grinning, arm slung easily over her shoulder, announced to the room at large that they'd finally found the best feminist in Delhi, and didn't she know it.

Manaswini smiled. She let herself believe it, the way you let yourself believe anything said warmly enough, by people warm enough, in a room that smelled of incense and cheap sweets and felt, for the length of that afternoon, exactly like the life she'd walked two hours down a mountain road in the dark to find.

Someone put a second piece of mithai in her hand before she'd finished the first. She ate it standing at her own desk, under her own name in its slightly-too-large font, and didn't think, for the rest of that day, about anything at all.
