# Chapter Nineteen: The Abandoned Factory

She surfaced slowly, the way you surface from a dream you can't quite place the ending of, and understood, before her eyes had properly focused, that something about her own body was wrong. Her wrists first - rope, not tight enough to cut but tight enough to remind her of itself with every small movement. Then the chair beneath her, hard-backed and cold through her clothes. Then the ceiling above her, corrugated iron rusted through in patches, grey light coming through the gaps in a way that told her nothing about what hour it was, or how many had already passed.

The room around her had the particular emptiness of a place people had once worked in and then simply stopped - rusted machine housings pushed against one wall, a conveyor belt gone still and skeletal, the smell of old oil and standing dust thick enough to taste. She tried her wrists once, testing, and stopped when the rope answered her with nothing at all.

"You're awake."

She knew the voice before she found the face it belonged to, and knowing it - Aniket, stepping out from a doorway at the far end of the room, unhurried, his shirt sleeves rolled the way they always were at the office - was somehow worse than a stranger's voice would have been.

"Aniket." Relief arrived before caution did, an old reflex she couldn't stop in time. "Thank god. Get me out of here, please, whoever's done this-"

"Nobody's done anything to you that you haven't earned."

The gentleness had gone out of his voice entirely, replaced by something flat and tired, a man reciting a case he'd already convinced himself of long before walking into this room. He crouched in front of her, close enough that she could see he hadn't slept properly in some time, and studied her the way you'd study a puzzle you were disappointed to have solved.

"I trusted you," he said. "I brought you tea on your second day. I told them you were the real thing, not another donor-call girl playing at feminism for a salary. And you were feeding information to whoever's been picking us off, one by one, since Akhilesh."

"I don't know what you're talking about. I didn't tell anyone anything - I don't even know what Akhilesh was involved in, not really, not until-"

"Not until your boyfriend killed him." He said it plainly, without heat, and the fact that he already knew - that this wasn't news to him, that somewhere in Aanchal's own reckoning they'd already connected Satya to Akhilesh's death - hit her harder than the accusation itself. "Zoya too, we think, though that one's murkier. Three of our own men, poisoned outside your very door. You want me to believe none of that runs through you?"

"I didn't know. I swear to you, Aniket, I found out the same way you did - after."

"After." He repeated the word like it tasted wrong in his mouth. "And you stayed with him. Kept sleeping beside the man who killed our colleagues, and told none of us, and just - stopped coming to work. Vanished, the same week Akhilesh did. You understand how that looks from where I'm standing."

She didn't have an answer that would survive contact with his certainty, and some numb, exhausted part of her stopped trying to build one. "Please," she said instead, smaller than she meant it. "Please, just let me go. I'm not your enemy."

The first slap came without warning, open-handed, hard enough to snap her head sideways and leave her ears ringing. She hadn't seen him decide to do it. There had been no visible escalation, no build of anger she could have flinched ahead of - one moment his hand was resting on his own knee, the next it had already landed, and only afterward did his face show anything like the effort it had cost him.

"Who are you working for?" His voice had gone hard now, urgent in a way that frightened her more than the slap had. "Who does your boyfriend answer to? Give me a name, Manaswini, give me anything, and I promise you this stops."

"There's no one. There's nothing to give you."

The second slap came harder than the first. The third she barely registered as its own event, folded already into the ringing that hadn't stopped since the beginning, her vision swimming, some part of her mind retreating somewhere the rest of her couldn't follow it to. Through it, distantly, she found herself remembering - absurdly, uselessly - the mithai in the stairwell, his hand on her shoulder telling her she was wasted on donor calls, should be running programmes instead of just paying for them. That man and the one crouched in front of her now didn't feel like two different people wearing the same face. They felt, worse than that, like the same person, entirely, both halves equally true.

"Please," she said again, the word barely a shape anymore by the fourth time it left her, and somewhere past the point where hope had any useful work left to do, she stopped bothering to make it sound like she believed saying it would help.

He didn't stop because she asked. He stopped, eventually, only because someone else's footsteps had begun crossing the factory floor toward them, unhurried, entirely unbothered by what they were walking in on.
